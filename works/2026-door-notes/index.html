Door Notes

by Annika Hiredesai

Door notes are deceptive little things. I liken them to Russian nesting dolls. What you see is rarely all you get, whether you are ready or not.

“Recent MS diagnosis, antidepressant medication reconciliation”

We are sitting together in her hospital room with the lights off, the shades drawn, the slits of late afternoon sun barely visible across the floor. Her exhausted father is asleep on the couch, curled up with his hands under his head as a makeshift pillow.

She can no longer even wipe her tears with her own hands. We laugh and cry at the sick irony. I do not know her faith. She does not know mine. Yet we pray. For the strength to let her children come see her, to let her fiancé care for her. Because her diagnosis does not mean God or love has forsaken her.

This is how my psychiatry attending finds us, my hands dabbing at her face with tissue, our silhouettes suspended in the fading rays of sunset, as he comes to see where I have disappeared all afternoon.

“Incoming trauma activation, concern for abuse”

Things are slow in our section of the pediatric ED that morning, so my attending sends me to observe the incoming trauma activation. This is my second one this week. I am feeling more familiar with the pacing, the waiting, the triple-checking, the watching of the doors with bated breath and limbs coiled. When the infant arrives, the fellow beckons me closer. I focus on trying to keep up with the team as they spring into action, assessing from head to toe. The words coming out of their mouths are vital signs and notable findings. I do not hear the story this child’s body is telling us.

We step out while the x-ray techs get trauma films. The fellow asks me about the signs of abuse on exam. I rattle off what I know: fracture patterns, presentations of chronic bleeds, mechanisms of injury. I note the outside head CT showed chronic subdural hemorrhages. Yet, something about her expression tells me I’ve missed the mark.

She asks, “What did you make of the absent brainstem reflexes in this child?”

I hear the blood rushing through my ears as I stare back at her.

This infant was brain dead upon arrival at our doors – maybe from the moment they were found abandoned on an apartment floor and airlifted to our hospital. With everything at our disposal, nothing could change that someone hurt this child enough to steal life. We were left in the aftermath. Nothing left to do but the workup.

I saw three other children present with physical or sexual trauma in the next 24 hours.

Never trust door notes.
