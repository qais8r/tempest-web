The Life Behind a Sample

by Shreya Gandhi

Research is not separate from the people that it intends to serve. In the midst of processing tissue, adjusting protocols, and analyzing data, we must not lose sight of the life behind a sample, the community this life touched, and the family anxiously awaiting the news. Likewise, caring for a patient’s needs in the clinic extends far beyond improving survival metrics. It requires a holistic, unincentivized approach that prioritizes patient comfort, solace to their caregivers, and timely access to clinical trials, therapeutics, and rehabilitation.

I learned this truth through a woman whose voice I still hear today.

Slow and steady, her voice was the sound of an enduring spirit that refused to be defined by statistics, but still helpless against a disease that had taken so many before her: glioblastoma.

I met her in my early days of brain tumor advocacy, when I was still navigating my own mother’s diagnosis and the lingering fear that at any moment, the tumor would return and I would lose her. She introduced me to the brain tumor community, welcoming me with open arms and drawing me in like a moth to a flame. And yet, the closer I came, the greater the risk of being burned. Perhaps this is the unspoken burden of loving those who are living with a brain tumor: the friends you make are fighting battles you can only glimpse, and every conversation carries the quiet understanding that they could be gone at any moment.

I remember the day I learned she was dying the way one remembers fever dreams: sharp, disjointed, and unreal. I was in the lab, labelling test tubes, listening to the steady hum of a nearby centrifuge warming to its set temperature, and preparing for an experiment that I had spent months planning. An unexpected notification changed everything: a recurrent tumor was being resected, a technician was bringing it down to the lab, and I was tasked with processing it for single-cell dissociation. Frustration rose quickly. All that preparation was gone; my experiment would have to wait.

I hope the tumor is at least large enough to make this worth my time, I thought. A fleeting, selfish thought it was. The kind that reveals itself only in hindsight, where shame lives.

When the specimen arrived and I began processing it, my lab mate approached: “Your phone is ringing.” I completed the final steps of mechanical disaggregation and enzymatic dissociation, a protocol I could perform in my sleep, and placed the test tube in the incubator on a rotator. I had thirty minutes.

I grabbed my phone. Twenty missed calls stared back at me.

It was a colleague from the brain tumor community. “I have bad news: she has been taken in for emergency surgery, and it is not looking good”, they said, their voice shaking.

I felt the blood drain from my face and my fingers go numb. My eyes shifted to the original test tube, now sitting in a biohazard bag, nestled among used latex gloves and discarded pipette tips. I peered at the name of the operating surgeon, black ink thinly scribbled on the side of the tube, likely written in haste; it was the same surgeon. I had processed her tumor.

The wish I had made earlier, I hope the tumor is large, echoed back at me like an accusation.

Dread. Disbelief. Guilt. Shame.

I visited her in the hospital shortly before she died, locking everything that had unfolded earlier in a Pandora’s box that I refused to open. I would not burden her with what had just happened, nor allow her to feel that her dignity or self-respect held any less value in my eyes. Even at her most vulnerable, she remained the woman who changed my life, who taught me true resilience and strength, and the flame that made every burn worth experiencing.

I think about her every day. The way we met, laughed, cried, and dreamed together; how she healed a part of me that I never realized was broken. She often talked about marriage, the life she imagined as a mother, and the love that she had for her family and friends. Would she have chosen differently if she knew that her first surgery would precede an inevitable recurrence? And that the multiple rounds of chemotherapy and radiation would bring pain and disability? Her lasting words, If I knew I was going to die, I would have lived, stay with me today, not as regret, but as instruction.

As I now embark on my own journey on the physician-scientist career path, I recognize that research and medicine exist in duality; each with perspectives that build on the other. Patients deserve to understand not only the treatments available to them, but the arc of the disease itself: what may change, what may be lost, and how each treatment avenue may impact the quality of a life that remains to be lived.

I will never forget the silent weight of that first sample, or the recognition that every piece of tissue carries with it a story that I may never fully understand. However, in learning the science and practice of medicine, I will choose to carry forward the humility and purpose that define it. One experiment, one datapoint, one patient at a time.

In many ways, a part of her lives on in each of us; every individual dedicating their life to helping someone breathe a little easier, stand a little taller, or face the next day with a little more hope.

Thank you for changing my life. I hope I honor you in all that I do.
